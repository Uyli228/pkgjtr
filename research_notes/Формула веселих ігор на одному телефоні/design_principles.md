# Design principles of fun local multiplayer / couch party games (the "secret formula")

> Method note: page fetching (WebFetch/curl) was blocked by the sandbox egress policy for every domain tried (gamedeveloper.com, builtin.com, co-optimus.com, iwataasks.nintendo.com). All findings below come from web-search result extracts of the cited pages, not full-page reads. Quotes are reproduced as the search extracts gave them; where an extract was itself secondhand (e.g. a review quoting a developer), that is flagged. The report writer should treat exact wording as "near-verbatim" unless a primary transcript is later checked.

## 1. What do designers of party/couch games say makes them fun?

### Takeaway
Designers converge on a few ideas: (a) **the real game happens between the people in the room, not on the screen**: look at each other, shout, negotiate, laugh; (b) **design for the watchers as much as the players**; (c) **instant readability with almost no friction** ("any player can grasp it right away"); (d) **failure has to be funny** (slapstick falls, a kitchen falling apart); (e) **leaving the system "incomplete" or "broken" on purpose**, so players referee and improvise, produces festive, performative play; (f) **laughter, not winning, is the real objective**.

### Cited Findings

**Douglas Wilson / Die Gute Fabrik: Johann Sebastian Joust, B.U.T.T.O.N., "intentionally broken" design**
- Wilson's PhD thesis (*Designing for the Pleasures of Disputation, or How to Make Friends by Trying to Kick Them!*, ITU Copenhagen, 2012) argues that "games which are intentionally designed to be confrontational, broken, or otherwise 'incomplete' can help inspire a decidedly festive, co-dependent, and performative type of play." — [Jesper Juul, The Ludologist](https://www.jesperjuul.net/ludologist/2012/10/16/doug-wilsons-phd-dissertation/); [full thesis PDF (ITU)](https://pure.itu.dk/files/109063787/Thesis_received_120927_Designing_for_the_Pleasures_of_Disputation_v3.pdf); [mirror](https://worrydream.com/refs/Wilson_D_2012_-_Designing_for_the_Pleasures_of_Disputation.pdf)
- Joust's rules "are intentionally open-ended in an attempt to encourage players to negotiate the rules and improvise new ones"; B.U.T.T.O.N. "pushes its players to roughhouse and cheat one another" and "coaxes them into playing the fool." The thesis frames this as an aesthetic of conflict that playfully reminds players "that togetherness is always provisional, never a given." It also builds on "abusive" game design (Wilson & Sicart 2010). — [Thesis PDF](https://pure.itu.dk/files/109063787/Thesis_received_120927_Designing_for_the_Pleasures_of_Disputation_v3.pdf); [DeepFUN summary](https://www.deepfun.com/designing-for-the-pleasures-of-disputation/)
- GDC Europe 2011 talk: "Intentionally Broken Game Design and the Art of 'Deputizing' Players". Wilson says he does not worry about enforcing rules or preventing cheating in his local multiplayer games. Players are "deputized" as referees, drawing on lessons from traditional folk games. — [Game Developer: GDC Vault adds free Wilson talk](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-vault-adds-free-jon-blow-douglas-wilson-i-zombie-lane-i-talks); [Game Developer: "Broken game design: it's not all bad"](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/video-broken-game-design---it-s-not-all-bad); [GDC Vault, "Folk Games, Festivity and Subversive Game Design"](https://gdcvault.com/play/1015630/contactUs)
- Joust has no screen, so "players end up looking at each other, moving around the entire physical space." The press kit lists "Players look at one another" as a feature. Wilson: "it's also really fun to see the facial expressions of your friends and to no longer be so tethered to this strange screen." He wanted a game "as fun for spectators as it is for the players themselves." Groups "start negotiating and enforcing their own social rules on top of the computer system." — [J.S. Joust presskit](http://www.jsjoust.com/presskit/); [Slate](https://slate.com/culture/2015/01/video-games-without-screens-johann-sebastian-joust-uses-a-playstation-but-is-more-like-a-playground-game.html); [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/archive/2012/12/05/sportsfriends-johann-sebastian-joust-screen-free-fun-for-ps-move)
- Joust mechanic: last player standing. You eliminate others by jostling their PS Move controller while keeping your own still, and the music tempo changes how much movement is allowed. — [Slate](https://slate.com/culture/2015/01/video-games-without-screens-johann-sebastian-joust-uses-a-playstation-but-is-more-like-a-playground-game.html); [Shacknews IndieCade 2011](https://www.shacknews.com/article/70546/indiecade-2011-johann-sebastian-joust)
- Joust won the Innovation Award at the 2012 Game Developers Choice Awards. — [NYU Game Center](https://gamecenter.nyu.edu/?p=5020)

**WarioWare: Goro Abe / Nintendo (microgames)**
- Abe (in the series from the start as programmer, designer and director; chief director of *Move It!*) said microgame controls and rules should be intuitive enough that any player can grasp them right away. — [Nintendo Life interview](https://3dsware.nintendolife.com/features/butt-movements-pitches-and-9-volts-retro-microgames-we-speak-with-wariowares-chief-director); the Polygon interview is cited via search summary only and was not opened.
- When selecting microgames for *WarioWare Gold*, Abe ranked them by staff appreciation, **how easy their rules were to grasp**, and whether they still felt current. — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-unusual-effort-that-went-into-creating-warioware-go-1829840289)
- The "Gold Digger" (nose-picking) microgame began as a design note that was just a picture of a nose, a finger, and the single word "Pick!". Abe turned it into a timing game: press A to push the finger into the nose. This shows the "one picture + one verb" spec. — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-unusual-effort-that-went-into-creating-warioware-go-1829840289); [GoNintendo](https://gonintendo.com/stories/320291-how-warioware-s-most-infamous-microgame-came-to-be)
- *Move It!* drew on 1,000+ staff ideas, and 223 microgame types made the final game. This is a high-volume, ruthless-curation pipeline. — [GoNintendo](https://gonintendo.com/contents/30326-nintendo-on-how-to-make-a-great-warioware-microgame-says-over-1-000-ideas-were)
- *Smooth Moves* (Iwata Asks): because it was a home-console game, the team "often pictured other people in the room watching" and aimed to make it fun for them too. Abe said keeping the watcher entertained was "at the forefront of our minds." The target reaction was spectators calling the game **"ridiculous,"** described as the best possible compliment. — [Iwata Asks: Smooth Moves p.3](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/warioware_smooth_moves/0/2/)
- *Smooth Moves*: explaining each action "in a split second" was hard, so the team added short pose prompts ("Hold the Remote like this") before each game. Varied grips were added because a single grip limited the fun. — [Iwata Asks: Smooth Moves](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/warioware_smooth_moves/0/2/); [Nintendo World Report E3 interview](https://www.nintendoworldreport.com/interview/11588/warioware-smooth-moves-e3-interview)
- *WarioWare: Snapped!*: Iwata called it "more a tool for making funny videos than a game". Microgames were designed **backward from which gestures would look funny**, e.g. shaking your head side to side, "an action people rarely see themselves doing." The game is "fun for someone watching." — [Iwata Asks: WarioWare Snapped! p.3](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/ds/dsi/4/2/)
- I found no source confirming a canonical "5-second rule" stated by Abe. Microgames are commonly described as a few seconds long, but the exact designer quote was not found (see Gaps).

**Jackbox Games**
- The studio strips games to "as little friction as possible", and **"winning is secondary; the true objective is to get the group to laugh."** Phones act as controllers. Players join via a web code with no app download, and shared information sits on one screen while each phone can carry private information. — [Built In Chicago: "These Design Principles Made Jackbox a Party Game Phenomenon"](https://www.builtinchicago.org/articles/jackbox-games-design-party-pack)
- Allard Laban (CCO): the interface is simplified so it would not frustrate his own relatives, and he compares the experience to "a hybrid of passive and active media". — [Built In](https://builtin.com/media-gaming/jackbox-games-design-party-pack)
- Laban: Jackbox is always looking at "how to make each other laugh together". The player count is fixed early because it drives scope and balancing. — [Gamereactor](https://www.gamereactor.eu/the-jackbox-naughty-pack-was-inspired-by-the-dirty-minds-of-the-players-1430943/)
- Laban describes the studio's identity as accessible. More gamer-focused, complicated designs get tried and then pulled back. Party Pack 11 is a "return to our old values." — [Checkpoint Gaming](https://checkpointgaming.net/features/2025/10/chatting-with-mike-bilder-and-allard-laban-about-all-things-jackbox-games/)
- Designer Brooke Breit: the aim is "giving you the diving board" so groups make their own inside jokes. The studio has roots in Chicago improv. — [WBUR Here & Now](https://www.wbur.org/hereandnow/2025/10/23/jackbox-games); [Boise State Public Radio](https://www.boisestatepublicradio.org/2025-10-23/jackbox-creative-director-on-party-games-roots-in-chicagos-second-city-improv-comedy)
- Quiplash makes **spectators participants**: the audience (up to 10,000) votes on answers and affects the outcome. Audience members can join at any time. — [PlayStation Blog (Steve Heinrich)](https://blog.playstation.com/2015/06/30/quiplash-new-party-game-expands-audience-participation-to-10000/); [Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/quiplash-review)

**Overcooked: Ghost Town Games (Phil Duncan, Oli De-Vine)**
- Duncan drew on restaurant work, where kitchens involve constant communication, working against the clock, and trying not to get in each other's way. He called kitchens a perfect analogy for co-op: "teamwork, time management, spatial awareness and shouting." — [Co-Optimus interview](https://www.co-optimus.com/interview/1838/page/1/indie-ana-co-op-and-the-overcooked-interview.html); [Wikipedia: Overcooked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overcooked)
- Duncan wanted the game "more about teamwork and coordination, rather than necessarily about individual players being particularly good at the game." — [Co-Optimus](https://www.co-optimus.com/interview/1838/page/1/indie-ana-co-op-and-the-overcooked-interview.html)
- "Panic" by design: **too many tasks for the number of players**, nuances at the upper levels, and many timers so the kitchen can unravel quickly. "The people who are good at our games are the ones who are good at communicating." — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/adventure/overcooked-broke-you-apart-and-stage-fright-is-there-to-put-you-back-together/)
- Playtest anecdote: groups of teenage boys played in **complete silence and failed the level horribly**. Communication is the mechanic, and without it the game fails. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/adventure/overcooked-broke-you-apart-and-stage-fright-is-there-to-put-you-back-together/); [Shane the Gamer](https://www.shanethegamer.com/news/stage-fright-wants-to-fix-what-overcooked-broke-devs-say-ahead-of-spring-2027-launch/)
- The studio now says Overcooked encouraged communication "maybe sometimes a bit too aggressively" ("Overcooked broke you apart and Stage Fright is there to put you back together"). This is a self-identified failure mode: co-op stress can tip into real friction. — [Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/interview-were-not-making-a-party-game-now-its-more-of-an-evening-thing-ghost-town-is-moving-on-from-overcooked); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/adventure/overcooked-broke-you-apart-and-stage-fright-is-there-to-put-you-back-together/)
- A widely circulated Duncan quote about wanting everybody "gathered around the TV and they're all screaming at each other and pointing" is secondhand (quoted in reviews). Original not verified. — [Hypercritic review](https://hypercritic.org/collection/overcooked-review-overcooked-2-coop-game)
- Postmortem exists: [MCV/Develop Post-Mortem: Overcooked](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/the-develop-post-mortem-overcooked/) (not read in full).

**TowerFall: Matt (now Maddy) Thorson**
- Built local-only for "the kind of intimate and frenetic experience you can only have when clustered close around the screen together with friends". The design feat is "**instant readability coupled with hooky depth**." — [Game Developer: Road to the IGF, TowerFall Ascension](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/road-to-the-igf-matt-thorson-s-i-towerfall-ascension-i-); [Game Developer: The magic of TowerFall: depth, simplicity, community](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-magic-of-i-towerfall-i-depth-simplicity-community)
- Thorson: "easy to pick up and play, but there's a lot of depth and detail to be discovered if you stick with it." — [Pocket Gamer](https://www.pocketgamer.com/towerfall/the-firing-line-5-questions-for-matt-makes-games-on-towerfall/)
- **One-hit kills** (borrowed from Bushido Blade) mean no complex moves divide players by skill, and every exchange is decisive. — [Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/culture/rs-gaming/maddy-thorson-towerfall-celeste-trans-stories-1234985446/)
- Competitive modes "just clicked" while single-player was the initial focus. — [PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/?p=126340)

**Nidhogg / Sportsfriends / Bennett Foddy (local multiplayer in general)**
- Mark Essen (Messhof) called *Nidhogg 2* a "spectator game" and chose a more detailed art style partly so onlookers have more to watch. — [Wikipedia: Nidhogg 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nidhogg_2)
- Nidhogg's frenetic feel drew crowds at events but did not carry over well online. — [Game Developer: "Play As Intended: A Case For Preferring Local Multiplayer"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/play-as-intended-a-case-for-preferring-local-multiplayer)
- A couch game is stronger when watchers enjoy it too. Foddy: good local games hold up over repeated sessions and are usually competitive. — [Game Developer: "The challenges and rewards of designing local multiplayer games"](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-challenges-and-rewards-of-designing-local-multiplayer-games)

**Fall Guys: Mediatonic (Jeff Tanton, Joe Walsh)**
- Root inspiration: *Takeshi's Castle*. The pitch opened with slides of Takeshi's Castle GIFs. **Physics comedy: Party Animals, Gang Beasts, Human: Fall Flat**
- Party Animals (Recreate Games) drew on Human: Fall Flat, Gang Beasts, Stick Fight and Smash. The game had to be "flat out fun whether you were winning or losing" while keeping depth for competitive players. — [GoombaStomp interview](https://goombastomp.com/party-animals-interview/)
- Reviewers note Party Animals doesn't make physics brawler controls "any better… I guess that's kinda the point." The clumsy controls are the joke. — [Finger Guns review](https://fingerguns.net/reviews/2023/09/18/party-animals-review-pc-beastly/)

Reviewers: controls are "simple and hilariously clumsy"; missing a jump is "a hilarious plop." — [TheGamer interview](https://www.thegamer.com/human-fall-flat-interview-tomas-sakalauskas/); [Nintendo Life review](https://www.nintendolife.com/reviews/switch-eshop/human_fall_flat)

**Ultimate Chicken Horse: Clever Endeavour (Richard Atlas)**
- Players build the level and race it. You score only if you finish and others don't, so each placement must "kill their opponents and grant them safe passage". This mixes cooperation and competition and drives a "one more try" loop. Party mode: a box of random objects, and each player places one before the round. — [Game Developer: Road to the IGF](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/road-to-the-igf-clever-endeavour-games-i-ultimate-chicken-horse-i-); [CGMagazine interview with Richard Atlas](https://www.cgmagonline.com/interviews/making-the-ultimate-party-game-an-interview-with-richard-atlas/); [Push Square review](https://www.pushsquare.com/reviews/ps4/ultimate_chicken_horse)

**Mario Party**
- Bonus Stars go out after the final turn, can "dramatically change the final standings", were in some versions skewed toward struggling players, and are random in later games. They can be turned off. — [TV Tropes: Mario Party](https://tvtropes.org/pmwiki/pmwiki.php/VideoGame/MarioParty); [Nintendo Players UK review](https://nintendoplayers.uk/news/review-mario-party-superstars/)
- Player reaction is split. One player disliked "how often I won by a hair's breadth solely because of the Bonus Stars," while a reviewer argued "half the fun of a Mario Party is feeling like the game is for or against you." — [Planned All Along blog](https://plannedallalong.blogspot.com/2024/04/mario-party-superstars.html); [But Why Tho? review](https://butwhytho.net/2021/10/review-mario-party-superstars-brings-the-franchise-back-bigtime-baby-switch/)
- Third-party design lesson: "if you're making a casual game, never let players lose hope." — [Brandon the Game Dev](https://brandonthegamedev.com/5-lessons-from-mario-party-for-board-game-developers/)

### Inferences
- Across Joust, WarioWare, Nidhogg 2, Jackbox and Fall Guys, designers explicitly name **the spectator** as a design target. For a shared phone, people not holding it (or waiting for their turn) are spectators, so every mini-game should look funny from the outside.
- "Laughter > winning" (Jackbox, Party Animals' "fun whether winning or losing", Fall Guys' "falling is always slapstick funny") suggests designing the **failure animation first**. The losing moment is the product.
- WarioWare's "picture + one word" spec ("Pick!") is a direct template for one-button microgames: one image, one verb, one timing decision.
- Wilson's "incomplete/broken" approach transfers to a shared phone. Physical crowding around one device (pushing hands, blocking views, the "rules" about touching the phone) becomes a deliberately unenforced social layer, as in Joust.
- Overcooked shows that **task overload relative to players** creates communication chaos. With one phone, contention for the single screen/button is a natural source of that overload.

### Gaps
- No primary source found for a Goro Abe "5-second" or "one verb" rule. The Polygon interview was surfaced but not opened.
- No primary NDcube/Nintendo developer statement on *why* Mario Party uses luck and Bonus Stars. The rationale is inferred by third parties.
- No primary Boneloaf (Gang Beasts), BombSquad (Eric Froemling) or Stick Fight design interview found.
- No transcript of Thorson on round length or match pacing.
- Full GDC talk transcripts (Wilson 2011; Overcooked; Jackbox) could not be fetched.

## 2. Academic research: social play, spectator/performer roles, laughter, co-located play, Caillois

### Takeaway
Research supports that co-located play produces **emotional contagion and "snowballing"** (spectators included), that **interdependence between players increases communication and lowers frustration**, while **shared control lowers felt competence/autonomy**. It also finds that splitting input across personal devices around one shared screen increases talk. Isbister's work shows spectators need to be able to read what's happening; when they can't, they disengage. Caillois's alea (chance) is commonly invoked as the "equalizer." That claim is widespread in design writing but not empirically tested in what I found.

### Cited Findings
- **Emotional contagion/snowballing:** Lazzaro (2004) and Márquez Segura & Isbister (2015) note the emotional contagion and emotional snowballing effects of co-located social play, extending even to spectators who watch players' backs. — [Isbister et al., Yamove! design case (Human Technology 2016), via ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303745033_Yamove_A_Movement_Synchrony_Game_That_Choreographs_Social_Interaction)
- **Framework for co-located physical social play:** Márquez Segura & Isbister, "Enabling Co-Located Physical Social Play: A Framework for Design and Evaluation." — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/278328625_Enabling_Co-Located_Physical_Social_Play_A_Framework_for_Design_and_Evaluation)
- **Spectator readability:** In Yamove!'s first playtest, "onlookers were not entirely sure of what was going on," and moves looked "somewhat socially awkward." The team added a human MC to guide players and crowd, and the final version works for players and spectators. A related paper: "How to stop being a buzzkill: Designing Yamove!, a mobile tech mash-up to truly augment social play" argues that the phone must not pull attention away from the social scene. — [Yamove! paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303745033_Yamove_A_Movement_Synchrony_Game_That_Choreographs_Social_Interaction); [Buzzkill paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266652709_How_to_stop_being_a_buzzkill_Designing_yamove_A_mobile_tech_mash-up_to_truly_augment_social_play)
- **Interdependence vs. shared control (Emmerich & Masuch, CHI PLAY '17):** "high player interdependence implies more communication and less frustration, whereas shared control results in less perceived competence and autonomy." They tested interdependence, time pressure and shared control in co-located play. — [ACM DL: "The Impact of Game Patterns on Player Experience and Social Interaction in Co-Located Multiplayer Games"](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3116595.3116606)
- **Personal devices + shared screen → more talk:** A shared-screen setup with multiple mobile devices produced significantly more oral communication than a typical console setup (N=40). — [Exploring Social Interaction in Co-located Multiplayer Games (CHI '13 EA), ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249314280_Exploring_Social_Interaction_in_Co-located_Multiplayer_Games)
- **Laughter as a measured social signal:** CHI '23 (Sykownik et al.) coded "mutually perceivable verbal utterances (e.g., conversation, laughter)" and gaze toward the other player when comparing sofa co-play with social VR. — [ACM DL: "VR Almost There"](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3544548.3581230)
- **Co-located cooperation → relatedness:** A CHI '25 within-subjects study (N=24) found cooperative co-located play increased team identification and relatedness. — [arXiv: From Solo to Social](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.00160)
- Co-located vs. remote: co-located play is assumed to give higher social presence, and an IEEE CoG 2019 study (N=92) tests it. — [IEEE CoG 2019 paper](https://ieee-cog.org/2019/papers/paper_19.pdf)
- Body contact as play material: "Bubble Popper: Body Contact in Digital Games" (CHI 2013). — [Exertion Games Lab PDF](https://exertiongameslab.org/wp-content/uploads/2011/07/bubble_popper_interactivity_chi2013.pdf); also "Designing Brutal Multiplayer Video Games" — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/293028466_Designing_Brutal_Multiplayer_Video_Games)
- Systematic review of social gaming. — [ScienceDirect (Computers in Human Behavior, 2023)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0747563223002029)
- **Caillois:** play types are agon (competition on an artificially leveled field), alea (chance), mimicry (make-believe/role-play) and ilinx (vertigo/disorientation), on a paidia (free, improvised) to ludus (rule-bound) spectrum. — [Wikipedia: Man, Play and Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man,_Play_and_Games); [Caillois short definitions PDF](https://voidnetwork.gr/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/Short-Definitions-of-Games-by-Roger-Caillois.pdf)
- "Alea is appealing, and randomness equalizes" (design blog remark, not a finding). Alea "negates skill" and is a "surrender to destiny." — [Game of Design blog](https://gameofdesign.blogspot.com/2011/10/agon-alea-ilinx-mimicry.html); [Dickinson blog](https://blogs.dickinson.edu/wrpg211/2016/02/07/roger-caillois-types-of-game-players/)
- Wilson's thesis (above) is itself academic (ITU PhD) and draws on Arendt's "action" and "plurality" for performance and togetherness. — [Juul summary](https://www.jesperjuul.net/ludologist/2012/10/16/doug-wilsons-phd-dissertation/)

### Inferences
- Mapping Caillois onto a phone party game: **agon** (reaction or timing duels), **alea** (random mini-game order, random modifiers, so anyone can win), **ilinx** (wobble, spinning, shaking the phone, frantic mashing, a light kind of vertigo), and **mimicry** (silly poses or voices, WarioWare *Snapped!*-style "do a funny thing"). Good party nights mix all four.
- Emmerich & Masuch's finding directly matters for one shared phone. **Shared control (two people on one input) lowers felt competence**, so give each player their own clear zone or turn. Keep **interdependence** (you affect me) high, because it drives talk and lowers frustration.
- The "buzzkill" warning suggests that on a shared phone the screen should be glanceable, so people keep looking at each other, not only at the device.

### Gaps
- No study directly measuring **schadenfreude** in co-located games was found. Depping & Mandryk (CHI PLAY '17) on cooperation/closeness was suggested but not checked.
- No empirical study found showing that randomness protects self-esteem or keeps weaker players engaged. It remains a design-folk claim.
- Isbister's book *How Games Move Us* (2016) is relevant but was not retrieved.

## 3. Specific mechanisms (usable as a checklist for 1–4 players, one phone, one-button mini-games)

### Takeaway
The transferable mechanisms with designer or research backing: instant readability (one picture + one verb), very short decisive rounds (one-hit kills, microgames), funny and legible failure, spectator-facing spectacle, direct player-on-player influence (sabotage, jostling, building traps for each other), mixed co-op/competition, light randomness or late-game swings to keep hope alive, deliberately unpoliced social rules, and physical or bodily comedy. Hidden information on a shared screen works best with private channels (Jackbox's per-phone info). On a single shared phone that needs workarounds (pass-and-peek, turn-away).

### Cited Findings
- **Instant readability / zero friction:** Abe's "any player can grasp right away" ([Nintendo Life](https://3dsware.nintendolife.com/features/butt-movements-pitches-and-9-volts-retro-microgames-we-speak-with-wariowares-chief-director)); ranking microgames on how easy their rules are to grasp ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-unusual-effort-that-went-into-creating-warioware-go-1829840289)); TowerFall's "instant readability coupled with hooky depth" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-magic-of-i-towerfall-i-depth-simplicity-community)); Jackbox's "as little friction as possible" ([Built In](https://www.builtinchicago.org/articles/jackbox-games-design-party-pack)).
- **Single verb spec:** "Pick!" plus a picture of a nose became a one-button timing microgame. — [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-unusual-effort-that-went-into-creating-warioware-go-1829840289)
- **Split-second instruction:** WarioWare needed short pose prompts because explaining an action "in a split second" was hard. — [Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/warioware_smooth_moves/0/2/)
- **Decisive, short exchanges:** TowerFall's one-hit kills. — [Rolling Stone](https://www.rollingstone.com/culture/rs-gaming/maddy-thorson-towerfall-celeste-trans-stories-1234985446/)
- **Funny failure:** "falling over was always slapstick funny" (Fall Guys) ([PCGamesInsider](https://www.pcgamesinsider.biz/feature/69833/how-mediatonic-made-the-takeshis-castle-of-video-games-in-fall-guys/)); "fun whether you were winning or losing" (Party Animals) ([GoombaStomp](https://goombastomp.com/party-animals-interview/)); clumsy controls as the joke (Human: Fall Flat) ([Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/reviews/switch-eshop/human_fall_flat)).

### Inferences: checklist for one-phone, one-button, 1–4 player mini-games
(Each item is a synthesis of the findings above, not a single source's claim.)
1. **Picture + verb test:** can the game be specced as one image and one word ("Pick!", "Stop!", "Tap!")? If it needs a sentence, cut it.
2. **Readable in under ~1–2 s by someone looking over a shoulder.** Big silhouettes, one focal point, the result shown huge.
3. **Rounds of seconds, not minutes.** Single-hit outcomes (TowerFall), so losers are back in quickly and nobody waits long.
4. **Design the fail state first.** It should be a slapstick payoff (flop, splat, explosion, ragdoll) that the loser laughs at too.
5. **Make the watchers laugh.** Ask "what will the people not holding the phone see?" Add spectator roles (vote, distract, judge) when possible.
6. **Direct interference:** let players affect each other (block, jostle, trap, steal timing). On one phone, the shared physical object is the arena: hands crowding one screen, Joust-style.
7. **Leave some rules to the humans.** Don't police everything, and let the group argue ("you touched my thumb!"). Wilson's "deputize the players."
8. **Each player owns a clear input zone or turn.** Avoid ambiguous shared control (CHI PLAY '17: shared control reduces competence and autonomy).
9. **Interdependence + time pressure** for co-op rounds (Overcooked): slightly more to do than hands available.
10. **Keep hope alive:** random mini-game order and occasional luck rounds, swingy late bonuses, a final "double points" round, so anyone can win any round. Make it **transparent and funny** (Mario Party's bonus stars are loved and hated).
11. **Mix Caillois flavours across the playlist:** agon (reflex duels), alea (lottery rounds), ilinx (shake, spin, mash), mimicry (silly poses or sounds).
12. **Near-miss tension:** timing windows and "stop at the last moment" games create big spectator reactions (inference from the WarioWare timing-game pattern; no direct source).
13. **Hidden info on one phone:** use pass-and-peek, cover-the-screen, or turn-away phases, since Jackbox-style private screens aren't available.
14. **Over-produce and curate ruthlessly** (WarioWare 1,000 → 223).

### Gaps
- No primary designer data found on optimal round length in seconds, or on near-miss tension specifically.
- No source specifically on "spectacle of losing" metrics or on one-button design for multiple players on one touchscreen. Items 12–13 are inferences.
- Betrayal/bluffing in couch games: no designer source retrieved beyond kingmaking and hidden-scoring board-game discussion.

## 4. What makes such games fall flat (failure modes)?

### Takeaway
Documented failure modes: silent play (no communication needed or happening), spectators who can't read what's happening, friction or complexity that excludes casual players, runaway leaders or "speed-bump" players who can no longer affect the outcome, kingmaking, luck that feels like it cancels skill, and co-op stress that tips into real conflict. Precise, skill-heavy controls can also remove the physical comedy, and online play loses the frenetic in-room energy.

### Cited Findings
- **Silent failure:** Overcooked groups playing in total silence failed horribly, so a co-op design that doesn't force talk falls flat. — [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/adventure/overcooked-broke-you-apart-and-stage-fright-is-there-to-put-you-back-together/)
- **Too much friction / tension:** Ghost Town says Overcooked pushed communication "maybe sometimes a bit too aggressively," and their next game is "there to put you back together." — [Nintendo Life](https://www.nintendolife.com/features/interview-were-not-making-a-party-game-now-its-more-of-an-evening-thing-ghost-town-is-moving-on-from-overcooked); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/adventure/overcooked-broke-you-apart-and-stage-fright-is-there-to-put-you-back-together/)
- **Unreadable for spectators / socially awkward:** Yamove! v1 onlookers "were not entirely sure of what was going on". — [Yamove! paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303745033_Yamove_A_Movement_Synchrony_Game_That_Choreographs_Social_Interaction)
- **Tech as buzzkill:** devices pull attention from the social scene. — [Isbister et al., "How to stop being a buzzkill"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/266652709_How_to_stop_being_a_buzzkill_Designing_yamove_A_mobile_tech_mash-up_to_truly_augment_social_play)
- **Complexity creep:** Jackbox repeatedly tried more gamer-focused or complicated designs and pulled back to accessibility. — [Checkpoint Gaming](https://checkpointgaming.net/features/2025/10/chatting-with-mike-bilder-and-allard-laban-about-all-things-jackbox-games/); [Built In](https://builtin.com/media-gaming/jackbox-games-design-party-pack)
- **Runaway leader / speed-bump players:** early game dominates and late stages don't matter, and a player who "can not win" is reduced to a "speed bump." Remedies include catch-up mechanics, standing-based rewards, and ending the game when players are out of the running. — [BGDF forum](https://www.bgdf.com/forum/archive/archive-game-creation/topics-game-design/tigd-runaway-leaderleader-bashing); [The Thoughtful Gamer](https://thethoughtfulgamer.com/2017/03/28/catch-up-mechanisms/); [BGG](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/1386029/stopping-the-runaway-leader)
- **Kingmaking:** a losing player picks the winner. This is especially likely in conflict-heavy multiplayer and with new players. Mitigations are hidden scoring, turn-order changes, and extra resources for losers. — [Skeleton Code Machine](https://www.skeletoncodemachine.com/p/is-kingmaking-cursed); [Skeleton Code Machine 2](https://www.skeletoncodemachine.com/p/kingmaking); [BGG](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/978837/problems-with-kingmaking)
- **Luck that feels unearned:** Mario Party Bonus Stars criticized for deciding games "by a hair's breadth." Some versions let players turn them off. — [Planned All Along](https://plannedallalong.blogspot.com/2024/04/mario-party-superstars.html); [Nintendo Players UK](https://nintendoplayers.uk/news/review-mario-party-superstars/)
- **Shared control kills competence:** CHI PLAY '17 found shared control lowers perceived competence and autonomy. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3116595.3116606)
- **Losing the room:** Nidhogg's frenetic local feel didn't translate online. — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/play-as-intended-a-case-for-preferring-local-multiplayer)
- **Over-tuned or under-tuned physics:** Human: Fall Flat's first fine-grained arm control was "too messy" and had to be reworked. Physics prototypes were dropped when results weren't achievable. — [TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/human-fall-flat-interview-tomas-sakalauskas/); [TheGamer 2](https://www.thegamer.com/risky-human-fall-flat-big-games-simple-physics/)
- **Instruction failure:** WarioWare found split-second explanation hard and needed explicit pose prompts. — [Iwata Asks](https://iwataasks.nintendo.com/interviews/wii/warioware_smooth_moves/0/2/)

### Inferences
- For a shared phone, the biggest risks are: (1) **downtime**, where 3 of 4 people watch a game that isn't funny to watch; (2) **unreadable outcomes**, where nobody can tell who won or why; (3) **one dominant player** winning every reflex game, which needs luck rounds and comeback scoring; (4) **cramped, ambiguous touch input**, where people argue about whose tap counted in a way that isn't fun. Deliberate jostling is fun; accidental input misattribution isn't.
- Luck should be visible and theatrical (a big wheel spin), not hidden in scoring, so that losses feel like fate rather than a skill verdict. This reconciles the "protect self-esteem" goal with Mario Party's backlash. (Inference; no direct source.)
